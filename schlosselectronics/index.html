The concept

A television you can almost feel

TV/VCR combinations from the late 1990s and early 2000s were made to be handled. You pushed a tape through a spring-loaded door until the machine took it, pressed a power button that clicked, and watched the picture come up out of static. I wanted to recreate that physical experience, not just the look of it.

SCHLOSS Electronics is a fictional brand I created for this object, with its own model number and identification label. Giving the set a maker and a model makes it read as a product someone could have owned, rather than a generic prop.

01 · Tactile

Details you’d touch

Buttons, a sprung tape door, a rear handle and a power cord. The parts people actually handled are the parts I wanted to get right.

02 · Recognizable

Forms you already know

A deep plastic body, a curved screen set back into the bezel, a slot for tapes and a small red light that tells you it’s on.

03 · Personality

A product with a voice

Electronics of this era carried their brand on the front. The SCHLOSS badge and the 3232 label give the set its character.

The larger idea

An interactive television that plays original illustrated VHS stories. Visitors turn it on, choose a tape, slide it into the VCR and watch what’s on it.

From concept to 3D

Building the set in Blender

The television was the first object I built, and the one the rest of the project depends on. The work came down to four things.

  • Object construction

    Treating the set as a manufactured product, made of the parts a real one would have: housing, screen, tape door, buttons, feet, handle and cord.

  • Proportions

    A CRT set is deep, and the screen sits inside a wide bezel. Getting the depth and the ratio of screen to housing right is what separates a CRT from a flat panel at a glance.

  • Screen curvature

    The screen is curved and recessed into the housing, so it catches light the way old glass did instead of reading as a flat rectangle.

  • Believable hard-surface detail

    Seams where plastic panels meet, screws, vents and jacks. Small, functional details that make the object look assembled rather than sculpted.

Modeling the details

The parts that make it believable

The overall shape tells you it’s a television. The small parts tell you it’s a real product: the controls on the front, the tape door, and everything on the back most people only see when they plug something in.

Building the VHS collection

Building the world beyond the screen

The television is only one part of a larger creative project. I’m building a collection of original VHS tapes, each with its own packaging, illustrated world and planned animated story.

That stretches the project across several disciplines: 3D modeling and physical product details, graphic design, environment illustration, animation planning and, eventually, interactive development.

VHS packaging

Modeling the cassettes and clear cases, designing the sleeves and developing a fictional movie identity for each tape. The cassette has windows that show the reels inside; the case has a plastic sleeve that holds printed artwork, the way rental and retail tapes were packaged.

Designing the stories behind the tapes

Metzengerstein: a Gothic castle chamber

For Metzengerstein, a short adaptation of Edgar Allan Poe’s story, I designed a Gothic castle chamber in Adobe Illustrator. The environment combines architectural elements, decorative textiles, period-inspired furnishings and a limited color palette to establish the story’s visual identity.

The chamber is one piece of a larger animation workflow, with separate environments, character illustrations and planned movement sequences.

What I’ve learned

Learning a tool by building one thing well.

I’m honing my Blender skills through this project rather than through standalone tutorials. Choosing a real kind of object gave the work a clear standard: I could always compare my model with how these sets actually looked and how they were put together.

The main shapes make it recognizable as a television. The small parts, like seams, screws, vents and jacks, are what make it believable as a product. Modeling those details meant paying attention to how things are manufactured and assembled, not just how they look from the front.

The project is also bigger than a single model, and breaking it into stages is what made it possible to start: the television first, then the tapes and cases, then the illustrated world inside each tape, with interaction saved for later. Writing this case study before the interactive part exists has helped too, because it makes me be precise about what is finished and what is still an idea.

Those are the same habits I rely on in product work: break a large idea into steps that can actually ship, and be honest about where each one stands.
